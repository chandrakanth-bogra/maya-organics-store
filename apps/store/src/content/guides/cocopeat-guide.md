Cocopeat (also called coir pith or coco peat) is the fibrous material that sits between the outer shell and the inner husk of a coconut. When processed correctly, it becomes one of the most versatile growing media available — renewable, pH-neutral, and excellent at both moisture retention and aeration.

## What makes good cocopeat

Not all cocopeat is equal. The most important factors are:

- Salt content: Raw coir is high in sodium and potassium salts, which can burn roots. Quality cocopeat is washed and buffered before sale.
- Particle size: A good mix has fine pith plus some fibre, giving structure without compaction.
- EC (electrical conductivity): Should be low (under 1 mS/cm) — high EC indicates salts haven't been flushed.
- pH: Ideal range is 5.5–6.5 for most plants.

## How to rehydrate cocopeat

Compressed cocopeat blocks expand 5–8x when rehydrated. Break the block into a large container, add water gradually, and allow it to fully absorb before mixing. Dry coir is hydrophobic — if you add it dry to a pot, it won't absorb water properly the first few weeks.

## Cocopeat uses

- As a standalone medium for seedlings and cuttings
- As the base of any potting mix (pair with perlite for drainage)
- In hydroponics as an inert substrate
- As a soil conditioner mixed into heavy garden beds

## Cocopeat vs peat moss

Peat moss is mined from ecologically sensitive bogs and takes thousands of years to regenerate. Cocopeat is a byproduct of coconut processing — India produces it in abundance. Cocopeat has similar properties to peat moss (water retention, slightly acidic pH) but is fully renewable. There's no reason to use peat moss when cocopeat is available.
